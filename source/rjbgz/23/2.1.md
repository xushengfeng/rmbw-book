（Adapted from The Red-Headed League by Arthur Conan Doyle）

Arthur Conan Doyle (1859—1930) was born in Edinburgh, Scotland, but was educated in England and Austria before returning to Edinburgh to go to medical school, receiving his degree in 1881. He later studied to be an eye doctor. Since Doyle had his own clinic, he had to attract his own patients in order to make money. However, he failed in doing so, and had to turn to writing to supplement his income. He wrote a number of short stories before finding modest success with his first novel, A Study in Scarlet (1887), which introduced the world to Sherlock Holmes and Dr Watson. In 1891, a magazine agreed to Doyle’s writing a series of short stories based on these characters. These made Sherlock Holmes a household name and Doyle a wealthy man. All in all, Doyle wrote four novels and 56 short stories based on Holmes, though he continued to write other fictions.

### Part 1

“Watson, let me introduce you to Mr Jabez Wilson,” said Sherlock Holmes one morning. Mr Wilson was an elderly, fat gentleman with red hair as bright as a flame.

“Perhaps, Mr Wilson, you would start again.”

“Dr Watson, I have a small retail shop in the City, in the alley behind the City and Suburban Bank in Short Street. I used to have two assistants, now I have just one, and I have him only because he agreed to work for me for half pay. His name is Vincent Spaulding. He is an excellent worker. However, he seems quite content to take what compensation I can afford.”

“A most unusual man,” I remarked. “There can’t be many good workers who would stay for half pay.”

“Oh, he has his defects,” Mr Wilson disclosed. “Well, only one really. He is an enthusiastic photographer. He’s always taking photos and then running down into the cellar to process them. But as the shop is not often busy, it is not a big problem.”

“Show him the advertisement,” said Holmes.

Mr Wilson handed me a square of newspaper. I scrutinised it with astonishment.

“This advertisement appeared in the newspaper. Spaulding showed it to me and said, ‘I wish I were a red-headed man.’ I didn’t know what to make of it at first, but Spaulding told me he knew all about the league. A rather strange gentleman, who had red hair himself, had started the league. When he died, he left all his money to the league to ensure it would continue forever. Mr Spaulding suggested I apply.”

“And so you did?” I asked.

“Yes. There were many other red-headed candidates waiting outside the offices. But somehow, Spaulding managed to push me to the front. When Mr Ross came out, he looked around the crowd until he glimpsed me. He told me I had just the right colour of flaming red hair and asked me if I would be free to work between 11 a.m. and 2 p.m. each day. I said that would suit me very well because my shop was never busy then.

‘There are just two rules,’ he told me. ‘You must not depart this building during your working hours. If you do, you will be expelled from the league. Secondly, you must keep this matter confidential.’ I agreed and started the next day. So, for the next eight weeks, I went to the Red-Headed League’s office at 10 a.m. and stayed till 2 p.m. All I had to do was copy out pages from an encyclopedia. At the end of each fortnight, I got my allowance. Then, this morning when I turned up, there was this sign on the door.”

### Part 3

At 10 o’clock, I met Holmes outside his house. With him were two men. The police officer, Peter Jones, I already knew. The other gentleman was Mr Merryweather, the manager of the bank.

When we got to the bank, Mr Merryweather opened the door, and we all crept silently down the staircase into the basement. The floor was covered in large square stones, and the room was filled with wooden boxes.

“That’s our French gold,” explained Mr Merryweather. “We borrowed it some months ago for a business transaction. As it happens, we didn’t need it, so it was never unpacked. It will be going back to France on Monday morning.”

“Please be quiet,” said Sherlock Holmes. “Hide behind the boxes and keep very still. We shouldn’t have long to wait.”

In fact, we knelt silently in the dark for over an hour. Presently, the glow of a strange light appeared under the stone floor. One of the stones began to move, letting more light in from below. Within moments, the stone was pushed on top of the next one, leaving a light-filled

shaft in its place. A hand appeared out of the shaft, then an elbow. A few seconds later, Vincent Spaulding was squeezed out of the shaft onto the basement floor. Then he turned and leaned over the hole.

“OK, Archie, give me the hammer and gear and then climb up yourself,” he said. At that moment, Holmes ran from behind his box and caught hold of Spaulding.

“Quick, Archie, run!” yelled Spaulding. We heard the sound of feet running away.

“He won’t get far, Mr Clay, for that’s your real name, isn’t it?” said Holmes. “Cops have been deployed at the shop door at the other end of the tunnel. They’ll catch him as he comes out.”

Once both thieves were in custody, Holmes and I went home to discuss the matter.

“And so you see, Watson, Spaulding, or Clay as his real name is, needed to get Mr Wilson out of his shop so he and his acquaintance could dig their tunnel without interference. When I knocked my cane on the pavement and heard nothing unusual, I knew they weren’t digging under the front of the shop. So I concluded they were digging out the back. When I made enquiries at the bank and found out about the French gold which would be sent back to France on Monday, I knew my hypothesis was correct. And I guessed they’d snatch the gold on Saturday evening because the bank would be closed the next day, and they’d have ample time to escape before the ‘robbery’ was noticed.”

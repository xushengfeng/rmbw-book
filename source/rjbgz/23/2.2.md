While mysteries are quite popular nowadays, authors have only been writing them for less than 200 years. In many ways, the mystery story made its debut with “The Murders in the Rue Morgue” by Edgar Allan Poe, written in 1841. This story introduces all the familiar fixtures of the mysteries we have all come to love: a brilliant detective who looks for clues to solve some murders, a friend who accompanies the detective and tells the story, and a final resolution where the detective explains who did it. While this all seems old hat to us today, Poe’s story caused quite a sensation when it was published. Keep in mind that in those days, urban police forces like what we have today were just being developed, and the word “detective” had not even been invented yet. It seems that Poe not only helped create a new literary genre, but a new way of looking at and solving crime.

In the 1880s and 1890s, Arthur Conan Doyle refined Poe’s ideas in his Sherlock Holmes stories, and by the time Agatha Christie became popular in the 1920s, mystery writers were already starting to agree on two prerequisites for mystery stories that most mystery writers adhere to even to this day: The readers should always be given all the clues they need to solve the mystery (a rule Poe violates), and there should be an abundance of red herrings—false clues—to distract the readers and add to the difficulty of the puzzle. A last rule is that the mystery should usually involve a murder, because a more trivial crime would not be able to generate as much tension.

So this is the who, when, and what of mysteries, but we still have not answered the why: Why do people have such an affection for mysteries, especially when they are so often limited by such rigid rules? One reason might be that they introduce readers to a world that they do not usually have access to. For example, Agatha Christie’s stories often involve the upper crust as they tour distant and exotic places. Through the stories, the readers get a glimpse into the glamorous lives of the rich, and discover that they have problems just like everyone else. Arthur Conan Doyle, on the other hand, explores the dark depths of London—a place which is foreign to honest citizens. Most modern mysteries follow Doyle’s pattern. However, there are twists. For instance, the Detective Wallander books have been popular in English-speaking countries in part because they take place in a foreign country—Sweden.

Perhaps another reason mysteries are so popular is because of the depictions of their main character, the ace detective. In addition to their analytical minds, these detectives are nearly always somewhat unusual, with odd habits and their own signature style. While in real life, you would probably not want to be friends with such people, in a book they make great companions, and you are always turning the page to read what they will do next. Of course, as integral parts of the stories, these great detectives often have companions of their own: Just as Holmes has his Watson, Poirot has his Hastings. While usually these friends are thought of merely as literary devices to give the detective an opportunity to explain his thoughts, they are much more than that: As opposites to the detectives, they provide character traits that the detectives lack. In a real sense, Holmes needs Watson to provide balance to his personality. Their witty conversations also make the stories more fun to read.

But these cannot be the main reasons mysteries are so beloved, can they? After all, there are many non-mystery stories which have exotic settings and unusual characters and which have failed to capture the public’s attention. That is the thing: Mysteries are popular because they are mysterious. Nearly everyone relishes a good puzzle. There is something fascinating about a story where all the clues are given to you, and you are given a chance to match your wits against a famous detective to solve a murder.

One would think that the most compelling mysteries are those where most readers can solve the case, and prove that they are just as intelligent as the detective. However, the opposite is usually true. People want to read a mystery that they cannot solve even though they have all the clues. The more elaborate the crime, the more interesting the story. Then when the solution is revealed, we find ourselves both surprised and delighted: The clues were all there. Why didn’t I see it?

Of course, the best mysteries are those that combine all three elements: an exotic world, an unusual detective, and a mystery beyond our own ability to solve. Such a book will keep most people reading until deep into the night. And then when they turn out the light and roll over in bed while pondering the mystery, just as they fall asleep they hear a dog bark in the distance of the closing of a door, and they wonder: “Are these yet more clues? Or are they just red herrings?”

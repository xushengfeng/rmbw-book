It was late afternoon when Judge Di knocked on the gate. When he announced that he wanted to see Mr Liang, a servant seated him in the corridor and disappeared. Sometime later, the servant came back and said, “Follow me.”

The servant led Judge Di down a corridor and through a courtyard. At last, they reached a door, and behind it a steep staircase. This led to another steep staircase, and then another. Soon, the elderly servant’s face was red and he was out of breath from the exertion—he looked like he was going to die.

Finally they reached the top floor of what appeared to be a tower. The servant motioned for Judge Di to sit down at a tea table in front of a portrait of the late Admiral Liang, Mr Liang’s father. Presently, Mr Liang entered the room.

“Would you like to have some tea, Judge Di?” Mr Liang said.

“Oh, I don’t mind if I do,” Judge Di answered.

Mr Liang smiled and poured them each a cup. Mr Liang’s cup had a small crack in it.

“I notice that you are a chess player,” Judge Di said, motioning to the tea table. On it was a chess board set up as though in the end moves of a chess game. The discarded chess pieces were in two brass bowls beside the board.

“Unfortunately, I can seldom find a worthy opponent,” Mr Liang said. “Though I heard that you are not half bad yourself.”

“I would like to discuss the theft of a woman’s body,” Judge Di began.

“What a curious thing to steal!” Mr Liang replied.

“Indeed. You see, I arrived here in Guangdong just a few days ago to investigate the governor’s murder. Strangely enough, it appeared that, from the time I arrived, I myself was being followed. My suspicions soon narrowed to a female dancer named Zumurrud. But she had no reason to assassinate the governor. It seemed clear that there must be someone else behind my surveillance and behind the murder, telling her what to do—like a chess player moving pieces on a board. Zumurrud, the Arab dancer confessed as much before she herself was murdered.”

“Murdered? The poor woman,” Mr Liang muttered. He got up and walked across the room to look out the window, with his back turned to Judge Di as though deep in thought. When he returned to his seat, he noticed that Judge Di was drinking from the cup with the crack in it, and his cup was nearly empty. Smiling, he picked up the other cup and drank all of its tea.

“But then her body disappeared,” Judge Di continued.

“Disappeared? No, no. She’s right here,” Mr Liang got up, walked across the room, and pulled back a curtain, revealing Zumurrud’s nude body. “I loved her. I worshipped her. However, she did not love me back, so in the end I used her. I had her kill others, and then I had her killed. Sometimes you have to give up your queen to win the game.”

“But why did you have her kill the governor?”

“The Empress had become angry with him, so we made a deal. I would help her with her problem, and she is to restore my house to its former glory by giving me a high position in court. After Zumurrud killed the governor, I had her stabbed so she would never leave me again,” Mr Liang said, stroking her face. “Besides, sometimes death is the best assurance of silence. Isn’t that true, my angel?”

“And so you will be assuring my silence as well?”

“I already have,” Mr Liang said, grinning. “And she provided the means.” He motioned to Zumurrud’s dead body. “She found me a poison which cannot be traced and which is always fatal. It is racing through your body right now. Within minutes, you will have died from this poison. They will say that you died of a heart attack because you are old and your body could not handle these many stairs. Your funeral will be glorious. And then I will be taking your place as a judge.”

“And how did you give me this poison?” Judge Di enquired.

“It was in your tea. I wiped it on the inside of the cup.”

“Ah-hah! But I switched cups when your back was turned! So I was not drinking from my cup—I was drinking from yours!”

“But as I am a good chess player, I anticipated that you would switch cups, so I made sure that the cup with the poison in it was mine in the beginning.”

Judge Di smiled and said, “But I anticipated that move, so when your back was turned I switched not just the cups, but the tea as well. I poured my poisoned tea into the bowl of black chess pieces, and your harmless tea in the bowl of white chess pieces. Then I emptied the harmless tea into my cup, and the poison into yours.” Judge Di held up the bowls to show Mr Liang the chess pieces were wet.

A look of shock crept over Mr Liang’s face. Trembling, he got up and began walking to Zumurrud’s body. Then he grabbed his throat and said, “My queen! My queen! I come to you!” before collapsing to the floor dead.

Judge Di looked puzzled for a moment. He walked over to Mr Liang and confirmed that he was dead. He then looked back into the bowl of chess pieces in which he had poured the poison, and smiled. When he had told Mr Liang that he had emptied that bowl into Mr Liang’s tea cup, he had lied: The poisoned tea was still in the bottom of the bowl. Mr Liang had just not seen it. Mr Liang had not drunk poison after all.

“Believing that he had drunk poison, he must have died of fright,” Judge Di said to himself.

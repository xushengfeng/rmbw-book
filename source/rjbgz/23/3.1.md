We are on the threshold of a Fourth Industrial Revolution but many people do not even realise it. The First Industrial Revolution was one of water and steam; the Second, of electrical energy; and the Third, of electronics and information technology. These revolutions spurred rapid change, the development of new and exciting industries, and greater prosperity than ever before. However, they also brought along with them significant problems, as nearly overnight people found that the world had drastically transformed around them. Things they took for granted yesterday were no longer true today. Worse, whole fields of work ceased to exist, and many people were not able to transition to new jobs and new ways of life. The Fourth Industrial Revolution contains within it all the opportunities and challenges of the previous revolutions. Building upon the Third Industrial Revolution, in the words of Klaus Schwab, executive chairman of the World Economic Forum, the Fourth Industrial Revolution is “characterised by a fusion of technologies that is blurring the lines between the physical, digital, and biological spheres.”

This Fourth Industrial Revolution began in manufacturing. Machines began to utilise sensors to gather information about their environments, high powered computers to analyse situations and make independent decisions, and communication links with other machines to relay information. Now, the machines were no longer just workers on the factory floor—they had become managers as well. While humans could still monitor the factory and provide input as needed, everything became automated. This new development in automated processes became the heart of what is now called the Internet of Things (IoT). Unlike the Internet of People (IoP), which connects humans to the World Wide Web, the IoT connects machines, computers, and devices together, allowing them to collaborate and make decisions independently, without human interference. When the IoT is linked with the IoP and combined with innovations in AI, 3-D printing, biotechnology, and nanotechnology, we will find ourselves in a whole new digital landscape.

How might this landscape look like in practice? Just imagine having a house where all the devices, machines, and fixtures have their own sensors and computers, and are connected to each other and to outside devices through the IoT. Your bed and toilet monitor your diet and health. Your refrigerator and cabinets keep track of your food supply. Your personal computer notices food and restaurant ads that you lingered over while surfing the Internet. It analyses this information and sends it to your smartphone, which presents you with several menus for dinner. You choose the option you want. Your refrigerator orders the food from the supermarket, and this is delivered and stored in your kitchen by machine. Your stove confirms with your self-driving automobile your expected time home. It then 3-D prints a delicious and healthy dinner for you that is hot and ready when you walk in the door. Nearly all of this technology already exists and is in use.

Of course, this revolution is not just limited to smart houses. We already have smart factories, and the Fourth Industrial Revolution is beginning to make its mark on the telecommunication and transportation sectors. Most schools, institutions, and industries will be impacted by this revolution at some point sooner or later. As with previous revolutions, once the Fourth Industrial Revolution is in full swing, many industries will flourish as they become more efficient and require fewer human workers. This will in turn drive down prices and bring economic growth, opening up new markets, and lifting millions of people out of poverty.

There are distinct challenges involved with this Fourth Revolution, however. The first and most obvious is that many occupations will go the way of the dinosaur. Some people will find it difficult to adapt to new jobs, and for a time the world may be separated into a hierarchy between the wealthy digital haves and poorer digital have-nots. However, in the end there will be new jobs to replace those that disappear, and most occupations will be safer, easier on the body, and higher paying than ever before.

Another challenge will be a change in the nature of human relationships. Already, many people really only have online relationships. Now, with AI, in the digital world, it is becoming difficult to tell the difference between humans and machines. Could there be a future where many people will think they have healthy online relationships with others, when in fact they are only communicating with computers? Can there be true intimacy in a completely online world?

Finally, there is the issue of privacy in a world where everything is connected through digital technology. A smart house, for example, will compile information about every aspect of its dwellers lives. Companies, governments, or hackers might be able to gain access to that information and use it to their own advantage. With this in mind, will the Fourth Industrial Revolution make privacy a thing of the past?

Despite these issues, the Fourth Industrial Revolution is inevitable, and so we should embrace it. However, we should also start now working on solutions to the challenges it might bring so that everyone will have a positive future. While new technology often brings problems, with a little thought it can also provide remedies for them. Just as the first three industrial revolutions fundamentally changed our world, the Fourth Industrial Revolution is certainly reshaping it.

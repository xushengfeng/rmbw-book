We began to face a tragic challenge in 2020 with the spread of COVID-19, which saw infection and deaths throughout the world. While we mourn the harm caused by this pandemic, and praise the efforts of nurses, doctors, and scientists who fought to gain control over this peril, we are also reminded of epidemics from years past.

Perhaps the most dangerous of these was an outbreak of the pneumonic plague in Northeast China during the winter of 1910–1911. This epidemic had nearly a 100% mortality rate, with death usually occurring within four days after first symptoms. The outbreak, which started on 8 October, was spread by travellers along Northeast China’s railway lines, quickly infecting the whole region. However, the city which was hardest hit was Harbin.

In December 1910, the Chinese government dispatched Wu Lien-teh (pinyin: Wu Liande) to Harbin to investigate. Wu’s family hailed from Guangdong, China, but he was born in Penang, Malaysia, in 1879, when it was still a British colony. In 1896, he went to Cambridge University, England, on a scholarship, and became the first person of Chinese descent to graduate from there with a medical degree. He then studied for a year under Ronald Ross, a Nobel Prize winner, before doing research at the Pasteur Institute in France and then returning to Malaysia in 1903. In 1908, Wu became the vice-director of the Army Medical College in Tianjin, China. This was the position he held when the government sent him to Harbin.

After he arrived, Wu found himself hindered by Chinese customs regarding death, which prevented him getting permission from the families to do an autopsy on any of the victims. Finally, he was allowed to perform an autopsy on a Japanese woman who had died of the disease—the first autopsy in China—and through this he confirmed that they were dealing with the pneumonic plague, which could be spread through the air by people coughing, sneezing, or even breathing near others. Accordingly, he believed everyone should wear face masks. However, his findings were hotly contested by other doctors on the scene, who insisted that the disease could not be spread through the air, and that the best measures to deal with the plague were to kill rats and institute better hygiene. People defied Wu’s advice to wear face masks to their own harm. In fact, in early January 1911, a French doctor was sent to replace Wu, but he refused to wear a face mask while visiting patients. In a matter of days, he also became a victim of the plague. The French doctor’s death silenced Wu’s critics, and within days face masks became the first line of defence against the disease. We even devised a more effective kind of face mask for use by medical personnel, and this is thought to be the prototype for the N95 face mask which is being used in hospitals today.

Under Wu’s direction, the whole area was placed under quarantine as a precaution, and police began going door-to-door looking for suspected plague victims and put them in quarantine so that they could not infect other people. As all rail travel was stopped, they were able to use railway cars to house people under quarantine. People who were able to go five days without showing symptoms of infection were released. As it proved impossible to clean the plague hospital they were using, a new hospital was quickly erected, and the old one was burnt down.

A bigger issue was what to do with the dead bodies, as they presented a danger. In the Northeast, the ground freezes solid in the winter, making it difficult to bury anyone. As a result, more than 2,000 bodies were put aside so they could be buried in the spring. Wu was afraid that when the weather began to warm and the bodies began to decay, rats and insects would feed on them, causing the disease to spread further. The solution was to burn the bodies. However, social customs proved an obstacle to this. Finally, Wu was able to get an order from the Qing emperor that the bodies had to be burnt. With this in hand, Wu began to have bodies cremated.

These tactics appear to have turned the corner on the epidemic, and by March 1911, there were no more infections. In April 1911, Wu chaired one of the first-ever international medical conferences in Shenyang, China, on the topic of the plague. This became a model for future medical conferences and for international collaboration on important public health issues. His experience in handling the plague elevated him to the very top of the medical world in China, where he helped modernise Chinese medical practices.

As one of the inventors of the modern science-based approach to fighting disease, one legacy of Dr Wu’s work is the protocol used for dealing with epidemics. This approach begins with establishing the nature of the epidemic, and then placing the first priority on stopping the spread of the disease. Next, face masks and quarantine become the main line of defence in such a fight. Medical personnel follow Dr Wu’s example by continuing to work, despite adversity and the many obstacles placed in their paths, so that they can bring healing to their communities and stop the spread of disease.
